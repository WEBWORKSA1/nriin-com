Returning to India does not make all your worldwide income taxable overnight. The Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR) category gives most returning NRIs a transition window — if they plan around it.

Who is RNOR

You are resident but not ordinarily resident in a tax year if you meet the residence test and either:

  • you were non-resident in at least 9 of the 10 preceding tax years, or
  • you spent 729 days or fewer in India during the 7 preceding tax years.

Certain Indian citizens with Indian income above ₹15 lakh who are not taxed anywhere else, or who qualify under the 120-day visitor rule, are also treated as RNOR. Use the residential status calculator.

What to do with your accounts

  • NRE and NRO savings: re-designate as resident accounts once you return for good.
  • FCNR deposits: can run to maturity, then move to a resident or Resident Foreign Currency (RFC) account.
  • Foreign bank and brokerage accounts: may be kept; they must be disclosed in your Indian return once you are ordinarily resident.
  • Demat and folios: update tax status to resident and switch bank mandates.

Returning US citizens and green-card holders remain US taxpayers. Indian mutual funds you buy after moving back are still PFICs for US tax. Plan your Indian portfolio with both systems in mind.
