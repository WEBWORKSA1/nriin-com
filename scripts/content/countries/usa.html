The United States has the largest Indian diaspora investing back home — and the hardest rules. Two systems shape every decision: Indian fund-house policies driven by FATCA, and US tax rules on foreign funds.

What works for US residents

  • Direct Indian shares through a repatriable NRI demat account. Shares of Indian operating companies are generally not PFICs, and most brokers serving NRIs accept US residents for direct equity.
  • India-focused ETFs listed in the US — no Indian accounts, no PFIC, ordinary US tax reporting.
  • NRE and FCNR deposits — interest is tax-free in India but taxable in the US.
  • Portfolio management services (in India or GIFT City) that hold shares in your name.

What is harder

  • Indian mutual funds: most fund houses refuse US residents; those that accept may require paper forms. Even when you can invest, PFIC rules usually make the after-tax result poor. See the directory and the PFIC check.
  • ULIPs and Indian-listed ETFs: also PFICs.

Annual US filing checklist

  1. Report Indian interest, dividends and capital gains on your Form 1040.
  2. Claim the foreign tax credit for Indian tax withheld (Form 1116).
  3. File FBAR (FinCEN 114) if all foreign accounts together exceeded US$10,000 at any time — due 15 April with an automatic extension to 15 October.
  4. File Form 8938 if your foreign assets cross the thresholds for your filing status.
  5. File Form 8621 for each PFIC holding.

Indian side

Tax is withheld in India on sales and dividends; file an Indian return to recover any excess. Use a US TRC (IRS Form 6166) and Form 10F to claim the treaty rate on dividends and interest.
